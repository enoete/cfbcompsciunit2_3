You already know four data structures. Now use them like you mean it.

This isn't a re-teach — Lesson 1 built stacks, queues and linked lists from scratch, and Lesson 2 built linear search, binary search, bubble sort and selection sort. This lab is where you decide which one to reach for, when it actually matters, and watch what goes wrong when you reach for the wrong one.

Five short labs, each building on the one before. Every one of them: a real scenario, a decision you make yourself with reasons attached, a guided trace with hints if you're stuck, and a small piece of code to complete. Full derivations live in Lesson 1 and Lesson 2 — come back here to apply them.

Why this lab exists

Knowing how a stack works and knowing when to reach for one are two different skills. The first is what Lesson 1 gave you. The second only comes from being handed a problem with no label on it saying "use a stack here" — which is exactly what every real piece of software looks like.

A wrong choice of structure rarely crashes a program. It just makes it slow, or clumsy, or wrong in a way nobody notices until the data gets big. That's the gap this lab closes.

The kitchen

Plates come off a stack of clean plates from the top — the last one washed is the first one used. Orders go on a ticket rail in the order they arrive, and the chef works them left to right — first in, first out. A recipe card sends you to "see step 4," which sends you to "see the sauce card," which sends you back — a trail of pointers, not a fixed shelf position.

Three completely different real objects, three completely different structures, and nobody in that kitchen ever had to be taught the words "stack" or "queue" to use them correctly. That instinct is what these labs are trying to build in you for code.

How each lab works

  • The scenario. A real situation, not an abstract one.
  • Your call. You choose the structure yourself, with reasons — the wrong options are wrong for specific, named reasons, not just "no."
  • The guided trace. Step by step, with a hint if you want one before you answer. Get it wrong and you find out exactly what broke, without losing your place.
  • Your turn. A small, focused piece of real code to complete.

Undo / redo — the stack

You're building a text editor. Every action — typing, formatting, deleting — needs to be undoable with Ctrl+Z, most recent action first.

Trace it

Guided

Three actions happen, in this order: type "Hi", type " there", apply bold. Each one is pushed onto the undo stack as it happens.

Your turn

Code

A stack needs exactly two moves: push something on top, pop the top thing off. Fill in the four blanks.

Fill in all four, then press Check.
Carrying forwardEvery undo popped off in the reverse order it went on — last in, first out. A stack only ever touches one end. A support queue needs the opposite order. Next.

Guided

Code

Fill in all four, then press Check.
Look backInsertion sort's inner loop — shift right while the neighbour is bigger — is a linear scan for the correct slot. It's the same shape as Lab 4's linear search, just moving values as it goes instead of only comparing them.
